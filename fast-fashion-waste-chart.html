The real lever isn't price — it's how many times you wear it

A garment's footprint is paid once, at manufacture. Every extra wear divides that same cost by a bigger number. Wear a t‑shirt 100 times instead of 5 and its water cost per wear drops 20‑fold — the garment didn't change, the habit did.

The numbers behind the charts

How these numbers were built

Three published anchors do the work, so you can check every figure yourself.

  • Carbon. The European Parliament reports that EU textile purchases produced 355 kg of CO₂e per person from 19 kg of textiles bought in 2022. Divide one by the other and you get 18.7 kg CO₂e per kg of cloth — an intensity you can apply to any garment once you know roughly what it weighs. Shoes are the exception: they use MIT's direct measurement of 13.6 kg CO₂e per pair of running shoes, because a sneaker is mostly synthetic sole rather than fabric.
  • Water. Per‑garment total water footprints: 2,700 litres for a cotton t‑shirt (European Parliament, using Water Footprint Network method) and about 7,570 litres for a pair of jeans (UNEP's "around 2,000 gallons"). Other categories sit between those two anchors, scaled by weight and fabric.
  • The basket. 53 items split into a realistic year: 20 tops, 8 shirts, 7 pairs of trousers, 6 knits, 3 jackets, 5 pairs of shoes, 4 accessories. Buying less scales that same mix down, so the comparison changes one variable only.
Read the water numbers carefully. That 2,700 litres is a total water footprint: it counts the rain that fell on the cotton field, not just water pumped out of a river. Rain would have fallen anyway, so "saving" it isn't the same as leaving water in a reservoir. It is the standard way the figure is published and it is genuinely useful for scale — but a litre here and a litre from your tap are not the same thing. The carbon and textile‑mass numbers have no such asterisk.
And one honest limit on the whole exercise. Not buying a garment doesn't unmake it — the shirt on the rack already has its footprint spent. What these figures estimate is the demand you stop adding to, which is how manufacturing volume eventually falls. It is a real effect, but an indirect and delayed one, and it only holds if you don't simply buy something equally costly instead.
